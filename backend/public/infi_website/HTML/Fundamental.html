FUNDAMENTALS

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)

The azimuth resolution of conventional real aperture radar depends on the dimension of the antenna and on the distance from the target. To achieve very high resolution in azimuth, a very narrow beam is required.
The azimuth resolution of conventional real aperture radar depends on the dimension of the antenna and on the distance from the target. To achieve very high resolution in azimuth, a very narrow beam is required. To get a very narrow beam, extremely long physical antenna is necessary. It is very difficult to build an antenna of several kilometers in length and operate from space. Therefore, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) was developed to overcome the limitation of keeping long antenna in space.

SAR systems use an antenna of much smaller physical dimensions, which sends pulses from different positions as the platform advances, simulating a synthetic aperture by integrating the pulse echoes into a composite signal. Aperture means the opening used to collect the reflected energy that is used to form an image. In the case of a camera, this would be the shutter opening, for radar it is the antenna. SAR can provide better resolution than conventional real aperture radars.

By proper post processing important information can be extracted from the high resolution images acquired by SAR. The advantage of using Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is that the spatial resolution is independent of platform altitude. Therefore, fine resolution can be achieved. Generally images are acquired in strips as the sensor moves along its flight path. These strips are called as swaths (the area illuminated by the antenna which is elongated and elliptical in shape). To separate the objects in the range direction (perpendicular to flight path), radar echoes are separated using the time delay between echoes that are backscattered from the different scatterers. To separate the objects in azimuth direction (parallel to the flight path) Doppler frequency is used. Since time delay and Doppler frequency is used to separate the objects on the Earth surface, the resolution is independent of the distance between the radar and the target.

Range: The direction perpendicular to an orbit or flight path. Azimuth: The direction parallel to the flight path of an aircraft or orbit of a satellite. Swath: The area imaged on Earth's surface by one pass of an aircraft or satellite.
Range: The direction perpendicular to an orbit or flight path.

Azimuth: The direction parallel to the flight path of an aircraft or orbit of a satellite.

Backscattering

When the EM waves interact with matter, reflection, refraction and absorption may take place. After interaction of EM waves with the target the backscattering energy is quantified by backscattering coefficient.
When the EM waves interact with matter, reflection, refraction and absorption may take place. After interaction of EM waves with the target the backscattering energy is quantified by backscattering coefficient σ° which is defined as radar cross section per unit area. The backscattering energy depends on the nature of the surface with which the EM waves interact. There are mainly three types of scattering that can take place at the surface.

If the surface is smooth (calm water or roads), then the reflection will be like a mirror (specular reflection). The energy is reflected only in one single outgoing direction. For the rough surface, the reflected energy will be less and diffuse reflection (the ray is reflected at many angles rather than just at one angle) will be more. For very rough surfaces, reflection will be in an upward direction from the surface.

The backscattering coefficient depends upon the angle of incidence, wavelength, electrical properties (complex dielectric constant), and orientation angle of the target with respect to radar line of sight, polarization and structure of the target. As the incidence angle increases, σ° decreases. For smaller angles, the penetration of EM waves into a medium is more and thereby giving more information about the target.

Dielectric constant gives the information about the reflectivity and conductivity of the various materials. If the target is more conductive (moisture or water content is more), then the return will be stronger. Water, especially saline water, is very conductive and has a very high value of dielectric constant whereas the dry targets (dry soil) gives very less value of dielectric constant. Trees and plants have more moisture and act as good reflectors. The moisture content of vegetation differs depending on the atmospheric condition. Metals are good conductors and hence the metal objects like bridges, rails, and electric poles give high returns and appears as bright features in the images. Whenever there is no backscatter, features appear as dark in the image, like the case of specular reflection at calm water surface.

Types of Backscattering

When an EM wave incident on the Earth surface, it can have three types of interaction depending upon the structure of a target. It can be single (surface or odd) bounce, double (even) bounce and volume bounce.
When an EM wave incident on the Earth surface, it can have three types of interaction depending upon the structure of a target. It can be single (surface or odd) bounce, double (even) bounce and volume bounce. Single or surface bounce occurs generally due to the interaction with smooth surfaces, double bounce occurs due to man-made objects like buildings and volume bounce due to forest (or any vegetation). If the EM wave has simple interaction (single or double bounce) with a target, the scattered energy retains the original polarization of the incident signal. If the incident wave undergoes multiple scattering events in the medium, then the original polarization of the incident wave will not be retained. This is known as depolarization of incident waves. It generally happens due to thick forest, extremely rough surfaces and randomly oriented dense settlement which is not perpendicular to radar line of sight.

For smooth surface, σ° of HH and VV is strong and σ° for HV is weak. For rough surfaces or isotropic vegetation, backscattering coefficient for all the three polarization and σ° for HH and VV are almost equal. The surface roughness is relative to the wavelength of the EM wave. If the surface variation is very small compared to wavelength then the surface appears to be smooth in the image and if variation is comparable (more) with respective to wavelength, then the surface appears to be rough in the image. Smooth surface gives specular reflection and therefore appears dark in the image. The rough surface scatters the energy in all directions and gives maximum energy back to the radar and therefore appears bright in the image.

Polarimetric SAR (PolSAR)

Polarimetric Synthetic Aperture Radar (PolSAR) revolutionized radar remote sensing by providing detailed insights into the characteristics of objects and terrain through their scattering properties.
Polarimetric Synthetic Aperture Radar (PolSAR) revolutionized radar remote sensing by providing detailed insights into the characteristics of objects and terrain through their scattering properties. In comparison with the traditional systems which were based on single-polarization information, the main feature of PolSAR was its capability to both transmit and receive microwave signals in multiple polarizations, such as vertical (V), horizontal (H), and cross-polarizations (HV or VH). This technology allows the PolSAR to gain the total polarimetric scattering information, namely the amplitude and the phase of the received signal to conduct a detailed analysis of the scattering process. As a result, PolSAR improves the differentiation capability between various target types, such as vegetation, man-made structures, and natural features, making it a versatile tool for applications in agriculture, forestry, and land cover classification. With the launch of dedicated PolSAR satellites, the technology has become more accessible and widespread, enabling global monitoring and comprehensive earth observation. Ongoing research continues to refine PolSAR processing techniques, enhancing its capabilities in fields like terrain analysis, environmental assessment, and target recognition. As it evolves, PolSAR remains at the forefront of radar remote sensing, providing a valuable perspective on our planet's dynamic features and complex environments.

Types of Polarization

Polarimetric Synthetic Aperture Radar works by transmitting and receiving radar signals along different polarization states with the aim of gaining comprehensive insight into the scattering characteristics of the targets.
Polarimetric Synthetic Aperture Radar works by transmitting and receiving radar signals along different polarization states with the aim of gaining comprehensive insight into the scattering characteristics of the targets.

Single Polarization (Single-Pol): This uses either single transmit and receive polarizations of H or V; which gives single polarization basic scattering information.

Dual Polarization (Dual-Pol): It transmits in one polarization, and receives in two, for example, HH and HV or VV and VH, therefore providing more information about scattering mechanisms.

Quad Polarization (Quad-Pol): It transmits and receives in both horizontal and vertical polarizations and captures all combinations (HH, HV, VH, VV) for comprehensive polarimetric data.

Polarization Correlation

Polarization correlation in polarimetry explains the degree of correlation or relation between the polarization states of the radar waves.
Polarization correlation in polarimetry explains the degree of correlation or relation between the polarization states of the radar waves. Within the context of the polarimetric synthetic aperture radar, known as PolSAR, polarization correlation is a fundamental concept explaining all relations among combinations of polarization states. Polarization correlation is further divided into two forms: co-polarization and cross-polarization.

Co-polarization is the relationship between horizontal (H) and vertical (V) polarization states of the radar waves. In PolSAR, HH is horizontal transmit and horizontal receive, and VV is vertical transmit and vertical receive. These are co-polarized signals. High co-polarization means that the radar waves maintain a strong correlation in their polarization states.

Cross-polarization is the correlation between horizontal transmission and vertical reception (HV) or between vertical transmission and horizontal reception (VH). These channels represent cross-polarized signals. Low cross-polarization indicates that the polarization states are uncorrelated, which indicates to surface or volume scattering.

Scattering Matrix

A fundamental idea in the field of remote sensing is the scattering matrix, often known as the (S-matrix). It is a mathematical representation, usually in the form of a 2x2 matrix.
A fundamental idea in the field of remote sensing is the scattering matrix, often known as the (S-matrix). It is a mathematical representation, usually in the form of a 2x2 matrix, which shows how the polarization states of the radar waves change as they interact with the target.

S = [ S11 S12 ; S21 S22 ]

From the matrix, S11 represents the co-polarized return amplitude for the same polarization state (HH or VV). S22 represents the co-polarized return amplitude for the orthogonal polarization state (HH or VV). S12 represents the cross-polarized return amplitude from HH to VV or VV to HH. S21 represents the cross-polarized return amplitude from HV to VH or VH to HV.

Covariance Matrix

The C2 matrix is used in dual-polarization PolSAR systems. It represents the statistical relationships between the two measured polarimetric channels (e.g., HH and HV or VV and VH).
The C2 matrix is used in dual-polarization PolSAR systems. It represents the statistical relationships between the two measured polarimetric channels (e.g., HH and HV or VV and VH). The general form of the C2 matrix is:

C = [ C11 C12 ; C21 C22 ]

In the above matrix, C11 and C22 are the diagonal elements. C11 is the element that gives the power of the HH (horizontal-horizontal) polarization return, capturing the intensity of the radar signal in the HH polarization. C22 represents an element that corresponds to the power of the VV (vertical-vertical) polarization return, capturing the intensity of the VV polarization. C12 and C21 give complex covariance between the HH and VV polarizations. They carry both magnitude and phase information to provide even a sense of the co-polarized HH and VV radar returns' cross-correlation.

Decomposition

The target decomposition was first introduced by Chandrasekhar 1960 and later applied to polarized microwave by Huynen 1970. Coherent decomposition theorems use [S] matrix and considers [S] as a linear combination of several other scatterers.
The target decomposition was first introduced by Chandrasekhar 1960 and later applied to polarized microwave by Huynen 1970. Coherent decomposition theorems use [S] matrix and considers [S] as a linear combination of several other scatterers. A method for coherent target decomposition was presented by Krogager, 1988. His approach was based on the observation that any complex, symmetric scattering matrix can be decomposed into three components, as if the scattering were due to a sphere, a diplane and a right or left rotating helix.

van Zyl 1989 describes the use of an imaging radar polarimetric data for unsupervised classification of scattering behavior by comparing the polarization properties of each pixel in an image to that of simple classes of scattering such as even number of reflections, odd number of reflections, and diffuse scattering. Coherent target decomposition methods can only be applied to coherent scattering. Generally, the scattered wave is partially polarized and the user might be interested in the extraction of geophysical parameters from an area that exhibits significant natural variability in the scattering properties van Zyl 1992.

Cloude et al. 1995 and Cloude et al.1996 suggested the H-A-alpha target decomposition theorem. A three-component scattering model for polarimetric SAR data is proposed by Freeman and Durden 1998. After applying different target decomposition methods to the polarimetric SAR data, Alberga et al. 2004 classified the images using minimum distance, maximum likelihood and parallelepiped classifiers. A four-component scattering model was proposed by Yamaguichi et al. 2005 to decompose polarimetric synthetic aperture radar images. Circular polarization power is added as the fourth component to the three component scattering model which describes surface, double bounce, and volume scattering. This circular polarization term takes into account the co-pol and the cross-pol correlations which generally appear in complex urban area scattering and disappear for natural distributed scatterers.

Interferometry (InSAR)

Interferometry is a remote sensing technique by the imagery of radar for measuring deformation or topography at the surface through the interpretation of phase differences between two or more radar images.
Interferometry is a remote sensing technique by the imagery of radar for measuring deformation or topography at the surface through the interpretation of phase differences between two or more radar images of the same scene acquired from almost adjacent positions or at different times. The phase of a radar signal contains information about the distance between the radar sensor and the target surface. An interferogram results from comparing the phase values generated from two radar signals: the interferogram exhibits a change in distance from the sensor to ground for each of its corresponding images. This phase difference responds very sensitively to any kind of slight displacement resulting from earthquakes, landslides, or subsidence as well as to variations in elevation. Factors that contribute to the quality of phase information include wavelength, baseline distance, or the distance between the sensor positions, and the surface roughness. Interferometry is sensitive; minute changes on Earth's surface that are millimeters in distance can be measured, therefore it is a powerful tool in understanding Earth's dynamics.

Digital Elevation Model (DEM)

Digital Elevation Model (DEM) is a 3D representation of the earth's surface, capturing the elevation data over a geographically defined area.
Digital Elevation Model (DEM) is a 3D representation of the earth's surface, capturing the elevation data over a geographically defined area. DEMs are usually produced based on remote sensing techniques; in this case, this may be Synthetic Aperture Radar or Interferometric SAR (InSAR). These technologies use a radar signal to measure the distance between the sensor and the Earth's surface, enabling the creation of detailed elevation maps. DEMs are represented as a grid of regularly spaced points with elevation values assigned to each point. The quality and resolution of a DEM depend upon the source data, sensor characteristics, and processing techniques applied. InSAR computes elevation differences based on phase information of radar signals compared from two slightly different perspectives and produces highly accurate DEMs. DEMs are essential tools for understanding the surface geometry of slopes, contours, and topographic features and underlie much of geospatial analysis.

Speckle Filtering

Speckle filtering is one of the essential steps in SAR image analysis. The step aims at filtering speckle noise in SAR images while preserving all the relevant information and features within the data.
Speckle filtering is one of the essential steps in SAR image analysis. The step aims at filtering speckle noise in SAR images while preserving all the relevant information and features within the data. Speckle noise is inherent granular noise in SAR images. It occurs from the random constructive and destructive interference of waves scattered from multiple scatterers in a single-resolution cell. While speckle carries some information about surface texture, its presence can obscure meaningful patterns and reduce the interpretability of SAR images. Speckle filtering is a process of applying a mathematical algorithm for smoothing of speckle that maintains the edges, structure, and radiometric detail of the image.

Co-registration

Co-registration is the process of overlaying several images of the same scene taken with radar to the point that corresponding pixels represent the same ground location.
Co-registration is the process of overlaying several images of the same scene taken with radar to the point that corresponding pixels represent the same ground location. It is essential for applications like interferometry, time-series analysis, and change detection, where accurate pixel-to-pixel correspondence is critical. The process involves an initial rough alignment using metadata, followed by fine alignment using feature-matching techniques, such as cross-correlation or mutual information, to achieve sub-pixel accuracy. Proper co-registration minimizes errors and artifacts, ensuring reliable comparisons of phase and intensity across images. It is a foundational step in radar remote sensing, enabling precise analysis of surface deformation, elevation, and temporal changes.

Geo-coding

Geo-coding is the process of bringing radar images into a standard map coordinate system, this can be latitude and longitude or in a projected coordinate system, such as Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM).
Geo-coding is the process of bringing radar images into a standard map coordinate system, this can be latitude and longitude or in a projected coordinate system, such as Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM). This allows for a registration against geographic maps or other geospatial products so spatial analysis can occur at precision. Geo-coding is important for overlaying radar data over other spatial data layers like satellite imagery, vector maps, or GIS datasets. Overlay in such a manner facilitates easy analysis and interpretation of the radar data over real geography, hence the possible application in the urban planning area, disaster management, and environmental monitoring areas.

Radiometric Calibration

Radiometric calibration is the process of correcting Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data to ensure that the recorded radar signal accurately represents the reflectivity, or backscatter, of the observed targets.
Radiometric calibration is the process of correcting Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data to ensure that the recorded radar signal accurately represents the reflectivity, or backscatter, of the observed targets. This step is crucial because raw SAR data can be influenced by factors such as sensor characteristics, acquisition geometry, atmospheric conditions, and system noise, all of which can distort the true radar return from the Earth's surface. The calibration process involves referencing the radar data against known standards, such as corner reflectors or homogeneous surfaces with well-characterized backscatter values. System-specific calibration parameters, provided by the radar's manufacturer or mission, are also applied. Accurate radiometric calibration is essential for quantitative analyses, such as land cover classification, biomass estimation, and soil moisture retrieval, ensuring that SAR data is consistent, comparable across acquisitions, and reliable for scientific and operational applications.
